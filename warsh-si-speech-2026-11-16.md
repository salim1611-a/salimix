# Warsh — "Opening Remarks" on Super Intelligence (Nov 16, 2026)

Source: text pasted into this session, presented as Federal Reserve Board remarks at the Productivity and Jobs Task Force Conference (FRB / SF Fed). It is dated after my knowledge cutoff, so I could not check it against the Fed's site.

## One-line read
A framing speech, not a policy signal. Warsh treats "super intelligence" (SI) as a general-purpose technology that may raise trend productivity. He lays out three scenarios and says plainly that they are **not forecasts**.

## Core argument
1. **Ideas drive growth** (Romer). Ideas are nonrival, so growth can show increasing returns to scale.
2. **The headwind:** research productivity has fallen (Bloom–Jones–Van Reenen–Webb: research effort is up more than 20x since the 1930s, but output per researcher has dropped by more). Population growth is also slowing (Jones 2022). Together these support the secular-stagnation view.
3. **Why SI could be different:** earlier GPTs (the engine, electricity) did not automate the making of ideas. SI might, which would change both the method and the speed of innovation (Aghion–Jones–Jones 2019). If so, trend growth could sit well above its long-run average.
4. **Limits:** humans stay "the deciders"; risks to critical infrastructure (from bad actors and adversaries) are flagged.
5. **The Fed's role:** neither advocate nor opponent. Prepare contingency plans and ask the right questions about **magnitude, scope and timing**.

## The three staff scenarios (illustrative)
| Scenario | Growth | Labor market | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Broad-based boom** | Persistently above trend | Near full employment | Gains shared by labor and capital |
| **Disruptive boom** | Above trend | Unemployment above full-employment levels | Displacement plus frictions in matching workers to jobs |
| **SI disappoints** | Slowdown | Larger rise in unemployment | Capex and equity gains reverse; recovery comes without the productivity gains |

## Implications for policy and markets (my interpretation)
- **A productivity-optimist bias, carefully hedged.** "Productivity… in recent quarters… reasonably strong… hasn't yet broken out definitively." This is the classic setup for an argument that faster growth need not be inflationary, so it can sit alongside lower rates than a standard Taylor rule would imply. Expect this theme to come up again.
- **The labor mandate is the swing variable.** In two of the three scenarios unemployment rises. That pushes toward easing even while growth runs above trend, which is an unusual combination.
- **Financial-stability tail.** The "disappoints" scenario is effectively an AI capex and equity unwind. Flagging it in the Chair's opening remarks is notable and suggests valuations are on the Board's radar.
- **No near-term guidance.** There is nothing on the rate path, the balance sheet or upcoming meetings.
- **Institutional signals:** five Chair-convened task forces, the "One Fed / Fit for Purpose" branding and outside voices brought into the discussion.

## Questions worth tracking
- Will the staff scenarios be published with numbers (r*, potential growth, NAIRU)?
- Does the FOMC's longer-run growth estimate in the SEP move up?
- How will measured productivity data be read against capex-driven GDP?
- What will the other four task forces cover?
